After a painful event, the mind may become trapped in “if only” or “what if” loops. It is natural to review what happened, but repeated mental replay can increase guilt and distress without producing new understanding. Acknowledge the emotion and distinguish useful reflection from rumination.

Begin by understanding your pattern. Record situations that trigger anxiety, who is present, what you predict will happen, physical sensations, and what you feel urged to do. This separates the different parts of the experience and can reveal where change is possible.

TITLE: Handling Conflict Constructively
WRITER: Student Minds
SOURCE: https://www.studentminds.org.uk/advice-and-info
PROBLEM_TYPE: interpersonal
CONTENT:
Focus on behaviour rather than attacking the person. Describe the specific action and its effect, then request a change. This gives the other person an opportunity to respond without being told that they have a permanent character flaw. Avoid sweeping words such as “always” and “never”.

Use statements beginning with “I” to explain your experience without assigning motives. A practical four-step structure is: describe the behaviour, explain your response, state the specific change you need, and explain the benefit or consequence. For example: “When you leave during the discussion, I feel disappointed because we lose the chance to solve it. I would like us to stay and take a short pause if needed.”

Prepare to remain as calm as possible. Agree that either person may pause the conversation. Allow enough time, pay attention to tone and body language, avoid interruptions, and share relevant information clearly. Show that you have heard the other person’s position even when you disagree.

Commit to a solution while remaining realistic. Consider whether each proposal is workable and ask why if it is rejected. Do not force agreement or promise something you are unwilling to do. Make the final plan specific: identify responsibilities, actions, and timescales. A trial period and a scheduled review allow both people to adjust what does not work.

Seek impartial support when direct discussion is unsafe or unsuccessful. A university adviser, mediator, student service, or another uninvolved person may help both sides communicate fairly and confidentially. Safety takes priority over resolving a conflict face to face.
